How to Buy Gig Tickets in Japan as a Visitor

Buying tickets for a small show in Japan is often straightforward. For a bigger artist, it can be one of the hardest parts of the trip. Start with the venue's page for the show, because it normally says exactly where tickets are sold.

Advance or door?

Prices are often listed as 前売 (maeuri, or ADV), for advance tickets, and 当日 (tōjitsu, or DOOR), for tickets on the day. Door tickets usually cost a few hundred yen more. A smaller show may well have door tickets available, but they aren't guaranteed, and sold-out shows won't have any. It's a good idea to contact the venue before you make a special trip.

Where tickets are sold

Large Japanese ticket sellers include e+ (eplus), Ticket Pia and Lawson Ticket. Zaiko, LivePocket and TIGET are also common, especially for independent events and streamed or digital tickets. Some small venues and bands also take reservations by email or through a form, which you then pay for at the door. This is called 取り置き (torioki).

Lotteries and fan clubs

Popular shows are often sold in stages. A fan club pre-sale (先行, senkō) comes first, then a lottery (抽選, chūsen), where you apply and are told later if you won. Finally, any remaining tickets go on general first-come sale (先着, senchaku). Sell-outs are common before general sale begins.

Common problems for visitors

  • Japanese phone numbers: some ticket sites require one to register. Look for an English or overseas-customer version, since some sellers and promoters offer one for bigger tours.
  • Foreign credit cards: these can be declined. Many sites let you pay in cash at a convenience store (konbini) instead, using a payment number.
  • Picking up tickets: paper tickets are often issued at a konbini machine or counter, using a reference number. Digital tickets may need an app.
  • Named tickets: some shows check ID and don't allow tickets to be transferred, so be careful about buying from resale sites and always check the official rules first.

If you're stuck

Hotel staff may be willing to help with a konbini purchase, and smaller venues will often reply to a short, polite email. If a big show proves impossible, look for a smaller band in the same scene.
